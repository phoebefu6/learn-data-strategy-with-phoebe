Part 1 · covers three funding models and who carries the risk

Three ways to pay for a data bet

5 min live

There is no published standard for funding a data function; what follows is common practice in any organisation, named plainly. A data bet can be paid from a central budget line, charged back to the business units that use it, or funded in stages like a venture, one tranche at a time. The three differ less in where the money sits than in who loses if the bet does not pay off, and that difference decides which bets each model can carry.

LiveWho loses if the bet fails?3 min▶

Central budget is the simplest: a line in the annual plan pays the data team, and the business uses what it builds without paying. It suits foundations that serve everyone and that no single unit would buy, which at Keelson is CDQ. Its weakness is that the CDO carries all the risk and the business has no reason to say no to anything, so demand grows faster than the budget.

Chargeback moves the cost to the units that use the work. It disciplines demand, because a request now costs the requester. It also starves foundations: no head of claims volunteers to pay for fixing a record that pricing and finance will use too. Staged bet funding releases money one tranche at a time, each tranche tied to a gate that the bet must pass. The risk is shared: the sponsor never commits the whole sum at once, and the CDO can stop a bet early without it reading as a failure, because stopping was written into the plan.

Self-studyMatching the model to the bet3 min▶

Most organisations end up with a mix, and the useful rule is per bet, not per data function. A foundation that every other bet depends on belongs on a central line, because no unit will buy it and stopping it halfway leaves nothing usable. A bet with an uncertain payoff and a clear early signal belongs in stages, because the signal is what lets the sponsor release the next tranche with evidence. Chargeback fits mature, repeatable services whose users can see what they get, such as a reporting service a unit already relies on; it is a poor first-year model for a new CDO.

Two warnings. A chargeback set up before any foundation exists will fund dashboards and leave the crux untouched, which is session 3's "quick wins first" result arrived at through the budget instead of the ranking. And staged funding only works if the gate is written down before the money moves; a gate invented after the fact is a renewal, not a gate. How the work itself is delivered and tracked belongs to the Data PMO course ↗.

Real world

The platform nobody would buy. A familiar pattern in any organisation: a data team moves to full chargeback to show it is "run like a business". Within a year its order book is full of reports and extracts for individual units, each one paid for, and the shared customer record that every unit complains about has no budget, because each unit expects another to pay. The model was not wrong for the reports. It was wrong for the foundation.

Part 2 · covers leading against lagging value signals

The signal that moves first

5 min live

The value a board wants from a data bet, such as lower fraud losses, usually shows up a year or more after the money is spent, and at an insurer later still, because a claim's cost is known only when it settles. A lagging signal is that outcome. A leading signal is the earlier change that has to happen first if the outcome is ever going to move: people and systems actually using what was built. Every bet needs one of each, and the leading one is what a staged gate reads.

LiveWhy the leading signal comes first3 min▶

Fraud triage at Keelson works only if claims are scored when they are first reported. If a month after go-live only a small share of new claims carry a score, nothing downstream can change: the referral rate to investigators will not move, and confirmed fraud will not either. So the first thing Maya reports is the share of claims scored at first notice. It moves within weeks, it is fully in her team's control, and it tells Tom whether the bet is alive long before the outcome is visible.

The trap is to report only the leading signal and stop there. Adoption is necessary, never sufficient: claims can be scored and the score ignored. That is why each bet needs a lagging signal too, written down at the start with a date to check it, so nobody can quietly swap the outcome for the activity once the activity looks good. The mechanics of turning these into objectives and key results belong to the OKR course ↗.

Self-studyThree tests for a value signal3 min▶

It can be counted today. A signal that needs a new system to measure is a second bet. At Keelson, "losses avoided" depends on claims settling, which is exactly what the diagnosis said arrives late, so it is a lagging signal and must be read with that delay stated. It has an owner who is not the data team. The referral rate belongs to Priya Natarajan's claims function; if only the CDO watches it, nobody changes behaviour when it stalls.

It says which way is good, and by when. "Track the referral rate" is a dashboard. "The referral rate rises, and the share of referrals confirmed does not fall, by the second quarter after go-live" is a signal a gate can read. The direction matters as much as the number: a referral rate that rises while the confirmed share falls means the score is sending investigators more noise. How to read such numbers without fooling yourself belongs to the Data Literacy course ↗.

Real world

The hardest part of the job, by the role's own account. Gartner's 2025 survey of chief data and analytics officers (504 respondents, reported) found that 30 percent named the inability to measure the impact of data, analytics and AI on business outcomes as their top challenge. One survey, read through trade coverage because the press releases could not be opened, so treat it as a signal of where the difficulty sits, not a benchmark. The practical lesson: a bet with no leading signal written down at the start is a bet whose value nobody will be able to show.

Part 3 · covers the board ask

The board ask, on one page

5 min live

A board approving the first year of a data strategy needs to decide four things: the envelope, the judgement behind the choice of bets, the rule for stopping, and what it will watch. All four fit on one page. What does not belong there is a return on investment figure for bets that have not started; the money case for a single bet, with costs and benefits in money, is the work of a planned business cases and ROI course, and the bench from session 3 claims orderings, never returns.

LiveGive the range, not a point3 min▶

Session 3 reran the exact search 500 times with each weight drawn up to 20 percent either side of its agreed value. The same set, CDQ, FTR and CFT, came back in 173 of 500 draws, 34.6 percent. Read alone, that sounds weak. Read with the bars, it is a clear message: FTR is in the exact set in 352 of 500 draws, and the other two slots split between CDQ with CFT (321 each) and CAT with RET (179 each).

So the board ask does not say "these three are optimal". It says: "Fraud triage is first under any weighting we tried. For the other two we recommend the claims fix and fast-track, because they also answer the diagnosis; the alternative pair scores almost as well and does not." That sentence hands the board a judgement it can own, which is safer for the CDO than a single number the board will later hold her to. The method of weighing options in general belongs to the Decision Intelligence course ↗.

Self-studyWhy no ROI figure, yet3 min▶

A return figure for a bet that has not started is built from assumptions about adoption, process change and outcome, the three lanes in Part 2, multiplied together. Each is uncertain, and the product is more uncertain than any of them, yet it arrives on the page with two decimal places. Boards remember the number and forget the assumptions. The bench was built to claim orderings for the same reason: with constructed rows, which bet rises and which falls is defensible; what a bet returns is not.

None of this means the money case never gets made. Each bet will need one, with costs and benefits in money, before its later tranches are released, and that discipline is owned by the planned business cases and ROI course. What this page gives the board instead is a staged structure: a small first tranche, a leading signal that releases the next, and a stop rule agreed in advance. The board risks less, and learns more before it commits the rest.

Real world

The number that outlived its assumptions. A familiar pattern in any organisation: a programme is approved on a single projected return, the assumptions slide is in the appendix, and two years later the only thing anyone remembers is the number. When adoption is slower than assumed, the programme is judged against the figure, not against the assumption that broke. A range and a staged gate put the assumption on the front page, where it can be checked first.

Build-along

Funding Keelson's three bets

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Maya writes the funding page of Keelson's strategy document: how each of CDQ, FTR and CFT is paid for, what releases its money, and two value metrics each, one leading and one lagging. Everything below is constructed, including every metric and gate; where a target or a date is needed, the page leaves it for you to set, because your numbers will differ. If you have your own three bets, run the same steps on them as you go.

Start from what the bench gave you. Copy session 3's exact set and its fit to the envelope. The difference is the headroom, which you keep visible rather than spend.

★ Step 1 · the starting point (session 3 bench)Bets: CDQ claims data quality fixed at source (defense) FTR fraud triage score at first notice (offense) CFT claims fast-track for simple claims (offense) Cost: 960 of 1,100 (thousands, constructed) Team: 21 of 24 team-months Headroom: 140 (thousands) and 3 team-months, unallocated Dependency: CFT needs CDQ Range: same three in 173 of 500 draws; FTR in 352

Choose a funding model per bet, with the reason. Ask one question of each: if it stopped halfway, would anything usable be left? A foundation that leaves nothing usable when stopped goes on a central line; a bet with a clear early signal goes in stages.

★ Step 2 · model per betCDQ central budget why: every other claims bet depends on it, no single unit will pay for it, and half a fix is no fix FTR staged funding why: uncertain payoff, but a clear signal in weeks CFT staged funding, gated on CDQ why: cannot start until claims are keyed at intake Risk sits with: CDQ = Maya's line; FTR, CFT = shared with Tom, through the gates

Write each gate before any money moves. A gate names the signal, the direction, the date it is read, and what happens on a no. Leave the target values blank until you have a baseline from your own data.

★ Step 3 · the gatesFTR gate 1 read: share of claims scored at first notice direction: rising toward [your target] when: [date you set], a few weeks after go-live on a no: fix adoption, or stop and return the rest CFT gate 0 read: CDQ's share of claims keyed complete at intake when: CFT tranche 1 is released only after it holds on a no: CFT money stays unspent, not reallocated

Write two value metrics per bet: one leading, one lagging. The leading one is what the gate reads; the lagging one is what the board was promised. Name an owner outside the data team for each lagging metric.

★ Step 4 · six metrics (constructed)CDQ leading: share of new claims keyed complete at first notice lagging: hand reserve rebuilds per quarter; sources for one claims total owner: Ben, Elena FTR leading: share of claims scored at first notice lagging: referral rate, and the confirmed share of referrals owner: Priya CFT leading: share of simple claims routed to fast-track at intake lagging: days from first notice to settlement, simple claims owner: Priya

Pair each leading metric with its failure reading. For every lagging metric, write the reading that would mean the bet is moving the wrong way even while adoption looks good. This is what stops activity being reported as value.

★ Step 5 · the wrong-way readingsFTR referral rate rises while the confirmed share falls -> the score is sending investigators noise CFT more claims fast-tracked, but reopened claims rise -> the routing rule is too loose CDQ fields keyed complete, but reserves still rebuilt by hand -> the record is filled, not trusted

Assemble the funding page. One page in Keelson's strategy document, in the order the board reads it: the ask, the range, the gates, the signals, and what is deliberately not on it.

★ Step 6 · Keelson's funding page (draft)The ask CDQ, FTR, CFT: 960 of 1,100 (thousands), 21 of 24 team-months; 140 and 3 held back The range FTR first under any weighting tried (352 of 500); CDQ + CFT recommended for slots 2-3 because they answer the diagnosis Funding CDQ central; FTR staged; CFT staged, gated on CDQ's intake record Signals six metrics above, owners named, dates set Stop rule a gate read as "no" returns that tranche Not here ROI figures (each bet's money case comes before its later tranches)

Read the stop rule out loud. Maya walks Tom through the page and spends most of the time on one line: "a gate read as no returns that tranche". A sponsor who has agreed to that in advance can defend a stopped bet to the board as the plan working, not the strategy failing, and a CDO who has written it can stop a bet without losing the other two.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Keelson's CDQ fixes claims data at source, and every other claims bet depends on it. Which funding model fits it best?

A foundation serves everyone, so chargeback leaves it unfunded while each unit waits for another to pay. Stopping it halfway leaves nothing to show, which is why staged funding suits FTR and CFT better. An ROI for a foundation is the weakest number on any page.

2 · Six weeks after FTR goes live, which signal should Maya report first?

Losses avoided is the lagging signal, and at an insurer it waits for claims to settle. The share scored at first notice is the leading signal: it moves within weeks and the referral rate cannot move without it. B is activity inside the data team, not a value signal.

3 · How should the board ask present session 3's result of 173 of 500 draws?

The draws measure how sensitive the choice is to the weights, never a probability of success, which rules out B. Hiding it (C) or swapping it for a return figure (D) removes exactly the judgement the board should be asked to own.
