Learn Data Strategy with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Diagnosis before ambition

A new chief data officer is usually handed two things in the first week: a deadline for "the data strategy" and a list of projects other people already want. The temptation is to rank the list, add a platform, and call it a strategy. Tonight we do the step that comes first and is skipped most often: saying, in one paragraph, what is actually going on. Rumelt's kernel gives the shape, a 30-day listening tour gives the evidence, and four familiar failure patterns show what happens without it.

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Keelson Mutual is an invented mid-size insurer: motor, home and small-business cover, a regulator that reads its reports closely, a policy system older than most of its staff, and a claims system bolted on later. Maya Okafor joined six weeks ago as Keelson's first chief data officer. Tom Reyes, the chief operating officer, sponsored the role and has told the board a data strategy will be ready in 90 days. On Maya's desk sits a spreadsheet of 40 requests from across the company. Everything Maya does across the six sessions adds one page to Keelson's strategy, and every Keelson person and number is invented for teaching.

Part 1 · covers Rumelt's kernel, applied to data

A strategy is not a list of projects

5 min live

Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy / Bad Strategy (2011) argues that a strategy has a kernel of three parts: a diagnosis that says what is going on, a guiding policy that says how you will deal with it, and coherent actions that carry the policy out and reinforce each other. A list of 40 ranked requests has none of the three. It has no diagnosis, so it cannot rule anything out, and its order usually reflects who asked most loudly.

LiveThe kernel, in data terms3 min▶

Diagnosis is a judgement about the situation that simplifies it: of everything going on, this is the part that matters most, and this is why. For a data leader the trap is to diagnose the data ("our data quality is poor") instead of the business problem the data is blocking ("claims cannot be settled fast because the claim record is fixed only at the end"). The second version tells you which data to fix first; the first tells you nothing.

Guiding policy is the approach that follows from the diagnosis, and it works by ruling things out. "Fix the customer and claims records before building anything that depends on them" is a policy: it says no to several of the 40 requests on Maya's desk this year. Coherent actions are the few moves that carry the policy, chosen so each one makes the next easier. Session 3 picks Keelson's first three, and the bench shows why the order matters.

Self-studyWhere the kernel comes from, and how sure we are3 min▶

The kernel and the hallmarks of bad strategy in Part 3 come from Rumelt's 2011 book and his McKinsey Quarterly article of the same year, "The perils of bad strategy". Neither was read in full at source for this page; the three-part kernel is stated the same way across many independent summaries, so it is reported here, not quoted. Rumelt writes about strategy in general. Applying the kernel to a data function is our move, not his, and the generic frameworks belong to the Strategic Thinking course ↗.

One reason the kernel suits a new CDO: it puts a written judgement before any method. Most data-strategy templates open with a maturity assessment or a target architecture, which are methods. They are useful later, once you know which problem they are for.

Real world

The strategy that was a purchase order. A familiar pattern in any organisation: the first "data strategy" deck is a target architecture, a vendor shortlist and a migration timeline. Eighteen months later the platform is live and the business asks what changed for customers. Nothing in that deck was wrong. It answered "what will we build" before anyone had answered "what problem are we in".

Part 2 · covers the 30-day listening tour

Thirty days of listening for the crux

5 min live

A diagnosis is built from evidence, and a new CDO's best evidence is other people's pain. The listening tour is common practice for leaders in a new role rather than a published standard; what matters is its order. Start with the people who will judge the strategy, then the people whose decisions depend on data, then the systems themselves, and only then write.

LiveAsk about decisions, not about data3 min▶

"What data do you need?" gets a wish list. "Which decision did you make last month that you did not trust, and why?" gets a diagnosis. The second question also tells you who owns the decision, how often it is made, and what it costs when it goes wrong, which is exactly what session 3 needs to score a bet.

Keep the same four questions in every interview so the answers can be compared: which decision hurts, what you use today to make it, where that breaks, and what you would stop doing if it worked. Write the answers in the person's own words. Maya's notes from Keelson are in the build-along, and every one of them is a sentence about a decision, not about a table.

Self-studyFollow one claim through the systems3 min▶

Week 3 is the one most new CDOs skip, because it feels like detail. Pick one ordinary claim and walk it from the first phone call to the payment, sitting next to the people who touch it. Write down every system it enters, every time someone re-keys it, and the moment each fact (the customer, the damage, the amount) becomes reliable. At Keelson the walk shows the loss amount is only trustworthy at settlement, weeks after the decisions that needed it.

The walk turns complaints into a mechanism. "Claims data is bad" becomes "the claim record is fixed late and the customer exists three times", and a mechanism is something a strategy can act on.

Say out loud what you are doing. Tell the sponsor in week 1: "For 30 days I am listening and I will not announce projects. On day 30 you get one paragraph that says what I think is really going on, and you can tell me if I am wrong." It buys you the time, and it sets the expectation that the strategy starts from a diagnosis.

Part 3 · covers how data strategies fail

Four ways to skip the diagnosis

5 min live

Rumelt lists hallmarks of bad strategy, among them fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives (reported). Each has a data version that you will hear in your first month, often from people who mean well. Recognising the sentence is the first defence.

LiveWhy "become data-driven" is not a strategy3 min▶

A goal says where you want to be. It does not say what is in the way, so it cannot tell anyone what to stop doing. "Become data-driven by 2028" is compatible with every one of the 40 requests on Maya's desk, which is exactly the problem: it rules nothing out. The test is simple. Read the statement and ask which request it would reject. If the answer is none, it is an aspiration, and the strategy has not started.

The same test catches technology-first plans from the other side. "Build a lakehouse" rejects plenty, but for a reason nobody stated. Ask "what is going on that makes this the answer?" If the reply is about the platform rather than a business decision, the diagnosis is missing.

Self-study"80 percent of data projects fail", traced4 min▶

Nearly every data-strategy pitch opens with a failure rate. The usual source given is Gartner. The trail, as reported in trade coverage: Gartner estimated in 2016 that 60 percent of big data projects failed, and in 2017 Gartner analyst Nick Heudecker remarked, in what was reported as a tweet, that the estimate was "too conservative" and the figure was closer to 85 percent. That is an analyst's informal remark about "big data projects", not a published study of data strategies, and it has been quoted since as 80, 85 and 87 percent for everything from AI to dashboards.

"Data is the new oil" has a similar life: commonly credited to Clive Humby in 2006 (reported), repeated as if it were a finding. The useful half of the metaphor is the part usually dropped, that crude oil is worth little until it is refined, which is a cost. Use neither as evidence in a board paper. If you want a number about failure, use one from your own organisation's last three data initiatives, which the listening tour will give you.

Real world

What the role is expected to produce. Gartner's 2025 survey of chief data and analytics officers (504 respondents, reported) found 91 percent named creating an actionable data and analytics strategy as a primary responsibility, and 30 percent named the inability to measure the impact of data, analytics and AI as their top challenge. Read together, the two numbers describe the gap a new CDO walks into: the strategy is expected, and the link from strategy to measured value is where it is hardest. Session 5 returns to the second number.

Build-along

Keelson's diagnosis, in one paragraph

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Maya has finished her 30 days. Below are five lines from her notes, each in the speaker's own words (all constructed). Work from notes to themes to one paragraph, as she does. If you have your own organisation in mind, run the same steps on your own notes as you go; your paragraph will differ, and it should.

Read the notes as decisions that hurt. For each line, write the decision underneath and who owns it. A note you cannot turn into a decision is a preference, and it stays in the appendix.

★ Step 1 · the notes (constructed)Priya Natarajan, head of claims: "A customer calls about a claim and we find them under 3 IDs." decision: who to fast-track, and whether a claim looks odd Ben Hale, chief actuary: "Every quarter we rebuild reserves by hand from claims extracts." decision: how much to hold for claims not yet settled Elena Ruiz, chief risk officer: "The last audit found two sources for the same claims total." decision: what to report to the regulator, and sign Tom Reyes, COO and sponsor: "We have run five analytics pilots. None of them scaled." decision: which work to fund next year Pricing team: "We never stored the quotes customers turned down." decision: renewal price, by customer

Cluster into themes by cause, not by department. Two notes belong together when the same fix would help both. Priya's three IDs and Tom's pilots that never scaled share a cause: each pilot rebuilt its own customer list.

★ Step 2 · three themesA Cannot join customers Priya, Tom (each pilot rebuilt the customer list), Pricing B Claims data fixed late Ben, Elena, Priya (the amount is reliable only at settlement) C Wins stay one-offs Tom: a pilot proves value, then has nothing shared to stand on

Find the crux. Rumelt's word for the part of the problem that, once addressed, makes the rest easier. Ask of each theme: if this were fixed, how many of the others would get easier? At Keelson, A and B make C happen; C is the symptom.

★ Step 3 · the crux testFix A (one customer) helps B? partly helps C? yes Fix B (claims fixed early) helps A? no helps C? yes Fix C (scale one pilot) helps A? no helps B? no Crux: A and B together. C is what they cause.

Write the paragraph. Three sentences: what is going on, why it matters to a decision the board cares about, and what it explains. No method, no technology, no target.

★ Step 4 · Keelson's diagnosis (draft)Keelson cannot trust or join its customer and claims data: one customer can hold several IDs, and a claim's amount becomes reliable only at settlement. Every decision that needs either one early (fast-tracking a claim, spotting fraud, holding reserves, pricing a renewal) is made on a rebuilt extract. That is why five pilots each proved value and none scaled: each one rebuilt the same foundations.

Test it before you show it. Three questions. If any answer is no, rewrite.

★ Step 5 · the three tests1. Does it name a challenge, not a goal? yes: "cannot trust or join", not "become data-driven" 2. Would Priya, Ben, Elena and Tom each recognise their own note in it? check with each 3. Does it rule something out? yes: anything that needs a clean customer or an early claim amount waits until those exist

Sketch the guiding policy in one line, and stop. Session 2 turns it into an ambition statement once Keelson is placed between offense and defense. For now, one sentence that follows from the diagnosis.

★ Step 6 · first line of the policyBuild the shared customer and early claim record first, and fund only the bets that use them.

Read it to the sponsor before anyone else. Maya reads the paragraph to Tom and asks one question: "Is this what is going on?" If Tom disagrees, the disagreement is the most valuable thing the 30 days produced, because the strategy would otherwise have been built on two different diagnoses.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min

  2. Pick the one process you will walk. The equivalent of Keelson's claim: one ordinary transaction that crosses the systems that matter most.
  3. Find your organisation's version of a failure sentence. Which of the four in Part 3 have you heard this year, and who said it?

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these is a diagnosis in Rumelt's sense?

A diagnosis says what is going on and why it matters, which lets it rule things out. A is a goal, C is a method with no stated problem, and D is a slogan; none of them would reject a single request on Maya's desk.

2 · A vendor's slide says "Gartner: 85% of data projects fail". What is the honest reading?

The figure is reported as Nick Heudecker's informal remark that Gartner's earlier estimate was "too conservative". It may point at something real, but it is not evidence about your strategy; your own last three initiatives are better evidence.

3 · How do you know a guiding policy is doing its job?

A policy works by saying no. Keelson's "shared customer and early claim record first" puts anything that needs those foundations behind them. B is a list, C is a goal, and D is an action with no policy above it.
