Part 1 · covers DalleMule and Davenport's offense and defense

Offense, defense, and the balance between

5 min live

In "What's Your Data Strategy?" (Harvard Business Review, May-June 2017), Leandro DalleMule, then chief data officer at the insurer AIG, and Thomas H. Davenport of Babson College put the argument in their subtitle: the key is to balance offense and defense. As the article is consistently summarised (its body was not read at source, so this is reported), defense means minimising downside risk and offense means supporting business objectives. Neither is the right answer on its own; the strategy is the balance, and the balance is a choice.

LiveTwo jobs, two kinds of truth3 min▶

Defense is the work that keeps the company out of trouble: meeting regulation, keeping data secure and private, making sure a number means the same thing every time it is reported. Its natural product is a single source of truth (SSOT), one governed version of each key record, such as the customer or the claim, that everyone agrees to start from.

Offense is the work that helps the company win: pricing better, keeping customers, settling claims faster, finding fraud. Its natural product is multiple versions of the truth (MVOTs): views shaped for one purpose, built from the SSOT. The two are not rivals. Offense without defense builds fast on records nobody trusts; defense without offense builds a clean warehouse nobody uses. The summaries agree that the authors' point is the balance, and that the right balance differs by company.

Self-studyWhat the article says at source, and what is reported3 min▶

Read at source on the article page: the title, the two authors and their roles at the time, the subtitle about balancing offense and defense, and four cross-industry figures in its opening. The article reports that less than half of an organisation's structured data is actively used in making decisions, less than 1 percent of its unstructured data is analysed or used at all, more than 70 percent of employees have access to data they should not, and 80 percent of analysts' time is spent discovering and preparing data. The studies behind those figures are not named on the readable page, so quote them as "the article reports", never as a measured fact about your organisation.

The body of the article sits behind a paywall. The definitions above, the link from defense to SSOT and from offense to MVOTs, and the idea that the balance shifts with industry, regulation and competition are consistent across independent summaries, so they are reported here rather than quoted. If your board paper leans on the framework, buy or borrow the article and read the body before you cite a detail.

Real world

Four numbers, one on each side. Read the article's four printed figures as a pair of pairs. "Over 70 percent of employees can see data they should not" is a defense failure: access was never governed. "Under half of structured data is used in decisions" and "80 percent of analyst time goes on finding and preparing data" are offense failures that start in defense: people cannot use what they cannot find or trust. That is the article's argument in miniature, and it is the shape of Keelson's diagnosis too.

Part 2 · covers SSOT and MVOTs as a mechanism

One source, several truths

5 min live

"Multiple versions of the truth" sounds like the problem a data leader is hired to end. It is not, as long as every version is built from the same governed source and can be traced back to it. The claims team, the pricing team and finance need different shapes of the same customer, and forcing one shape on all three is how shadow spreadsheets begin. The rule is: versions may differ on purpose, never by accident.

LiveWhy three teams need three versions3 min▶

Take one Keelson customer, a small-business owner with a van policy, a home policy and an open claim. The claims team wants her grouped with her household, because two claims from one address in a month is a fraud signal. Pricing wants her with every quote she turned down, because that is what tells them where the price was wrong. Finance wants her as booked, period by period, because that is what the regulator and the auditors check. All three are correct, and all three are different.

What makes them safe is the arrow back. If each view starts from the same customer ID and the rules that shape it are written down, a difference between two reports can be explained in one sentence. If each view started from its own extract, the difference becomes a meeting, and at Keelson that meeting happened when the audit found two sources for the same claims total.

Self-studyHow to tell a governed version from a shadow copy3 min▶

Three questions separate an MVOT from a spreadsheet that happens to agree today. Does it start from the SSOT's key, or from a private extract? Are the rules that reshape it written down where someone else can read them? Does it have a named owner who answers when it disagrees with another view? Three yeses make it a version; any no makes it a copy that will drift.

The governance machinery that makes the SSOT real (ownership, quality rules, stewardship) is taught in depth in the Data Governance course ↗, and building one view as a product with users and a roadmap belongs to the Data Product course ↗. The strategy question is narrower: which records must become an SSOT first, because the most valuable views depend on them. Session 4 returns to the minimum governance needed for that.

Real world

Three revenue numbers at one board meeting. A familiar pattern in any organisation: sales, finance and the analytics team each bring a revenue figure, all three are defensible, and the first twenty minutes go on reconciling them. The usual fix, declaring one of them "the truth" and banning the others, fails within a quarter because the other two existed for a reason. The fix that lasts is upstream: one governed order record, three written definitions on top of it, and a one-line note on each chart saying which definition it uses.

Part 3 · covers placing an insurer on the dial

Placing an insurer on the dial

5 min live

The summaries agree that, in the article, the right balance depends on industry, regulation and competition (reported). For an insurer those forces pull in opposite directions at once. Regulation pulls toward defense: reports a supervisor reads closely, audits, capital calculations that must reconcile. Competition pulls toward offense: price, speed of settlement, keeping good customers at renewal. The position is a judgement about which pull matters more this year, and it is supposed to move.

LiveWhy the dial moves over time3 min▶

A position on the dial is not a personality. It is a statement about this year. Three things move it. A regulatory finding or a breach pulls it sharply toward defense, often overnight. A competitor that prices faster or settles claims in days pulls it toward offense. And the most useful mover is your own progress: once the single customer record and early claim data exist, the offense work that depends on them becomes cheap, so the same strategy that leaned defense in year one can lean offense in year two without changing its mind.

That third mover is why "we are a defensive organisation" is the wrong conclusion to draw from a regulated industry. Defense first can be the fastest route to offense, as long as you say so and name the moment the balance shifts.

Self-studyReading the forces in your own organisation3 min▶

List the last five things that reached the board about data. Mark each one as a defense signal (an audit point, a breach, a report that would not reconcile) or an offense signal (a lost customer segment, a slow process a competitor does faster, a pilot that worked). The count is not a score; the article does not give one, and no published scale turns these signals into a dial position. It is evidence for a judgement you will have to defend.

Then ask what would move the needle a year from now. If the answer is "nothing we plan to do", the strategy is static and it will be overtaken by events. If the answer names a piece of defense work that unlocks offense, you have the start of a sequence, which is exactly what session 3 turns into three bets.

Real world

The swing after a finding. A familiar pattern in regulated firms: an audit finding lands, every data budget swings to remediation, and the analytics team spends a year reconciling reports. When the finding closes, the budget swings back and the foundations are left half-built. A dial position written down before the finding, with its reason, lets a CDO argue for keeping the defense work that the offense plan needs, rather than swinging twice.

Build-along

Keelson on the dial, and an ambition it can keep

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Maya places Keelson on the dial using only evidence from her session 1 notes (all constructed), then writes a two-sentence ambition statement and tests it against Rumelt's "mistaking goals for strategy" (reported). Run the same steps on your own diagnosis as you go; your position will differ, and the reasons matter more than the spot.

Sort the evidence by the way it pulls. Take each note from your diagnosis and ask: is this about risk to the company, or about value the company is missing? Write one word against each, defense or offense. Do not add new evidence yet; the dial should rest on what the listening tour already found.

★ Step 1 · the evidence, sorted (constructed)Elena: audit found two sources for one claims total DEFENSE Priya: one customer held under 3 IDs DEFENSE Ben: reserves rebuilt by hand every quarter DEFENSE Tom: five pilots proved value, none scaled OFFENSE Pricing: declined quotes were never stored OFFENSE

Name the forces outside the building. Two lines: what the regulator expects of Keelson's data, and where competitors are beating it. Keep both qualitative; Keelson is constructed and there is no market statistic to quote.

★ Step 2 · the two forcesRegulation: claims totals and reserves must reconcile in every report, and the last audit says they do not. pulls DEFENSE Competition: rivals settle simple claims faster and price renewals per customer. pulls OFFENSE

Ask which pull is the cause. This is the step that turns a tally into a judgement. At Keelson the defense notes explain the offense notes: pilots could not scale because each rebuilt the customer, and pricing cannot use declined quotes it cannot join to a customer. When defense is the cause of the offense pain, the dial leans defense, and says why.

★ Step 3 · cause and effectDefense gap blocks offense win one customer, 3 IDs -> pilots cannot scale claims amount fixed late -> fast-track, fraud triage no governed customer key -> renewal pricing by customer Verdict: the offense pain is downstream of the defense gap.

Place the needle today and say where it moves. One sentence for today, one for the move, and the event that triggers it. The trigger matters most: it is the promise that defense is a route, not a destination.

★ Step 4 · Keelson on the dialToday: lean defense. Build the single customer record and capture claim facts at first notice. Move: toward balance once claims data is fixed at source (session 3 calls this bet CDQ). Trigger: the claims total reconciles in two quarters running, with no manual rebuild.

Write the ambition in two sentences. Sentence one says what comes first and why. Sentence two says what that unlocks and which offense work waits for it. No target year, no percentage, no adjective doing the work of a decision.

★ Step 5 · Keelson's ambition statement (draft)For the next two years Keelson's data work leans defense: one governed customer record and claim facts captured at first notice, owned and audited. Every offense bet we fund must stand on those two records, starting with the claims decisions that need them earliest.

Test it against "goals are not strategy". Rumelt's hallmark (reported) is mistaking a goal for a strategy. The test from session 1 still works: read the statement and name the requests it rejects. If it rejects nothing, rewrite it.

★ Step 6 · the goal testRejects, for now: a renewal-pricing model on today's three customer IDs a claims chatbot built on raw extracts a new platform bought before the records it will hold exist Allows: any bet that uses the customer record or early claim facts Compare: "Become a data-driven insurer by 2028" rejects nothing. It is a goal.

Read the trigger to the sponsor, not just the position. Tom does not need to agree that Keelson is "defensive". He needs to agree on the event that moves the needle, because that is what he will be asked about at the board. A position without a trigger reads as caution; a position with one reads as a plan.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · How do a single source of truth and multiple versions of the truth relate?

As the article is summarised, defense is tied to the SSOT and offense to MVOTs built from it. Several versions are healthy when they differ on purpose and share one governed key; C reverses the sides, and A and B lose either trust or usefulness.

2 · Which event is most likely to move an insurer's dial toward defense this year?

A reconciliation failure in a regulated report is a defense signal: regulation pulls toward control. A and D are offense signals, and a request for a pilot (C) is a wish, not a force on the balance.

3 · Which ambition statement passes the "goals are not strategy" test?

C says what comes first and why, and it rules out bets that need records which do not exist yet. A and B are goals that reject nothing, and D is a list of inputs with no diagnosis behind it.
